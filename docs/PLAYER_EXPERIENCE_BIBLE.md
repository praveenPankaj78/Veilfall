# Player Experience Bible

## Purpose

This document controls what the player understands, expects, and wonders about while playing. The world bible may contain deep history, but the game reveals only what helps the player interpret the present scene or choose the next action.

The desired feeling is informed uncertainty. The player should understand the problem in front of them while remaining curious about its larger meaning.

## The three player questions

At every choice, the player should be able to answer:

1. What am I trying to achieve right now?
2. What will this choice probably cost immediately?
3. Why might I prefer this approach as this character?

At every chapter ending, the player should be able to answer:

1. What did I accomplish or fail to protect?
2. What did I learn that I did not know before?
3. What do I want to do next?

If a playtester cannot answer any one of these, the chapter needs revision.

## Curiosity and confusion

Curiosity means the player has a focused question and enough evidence to imagine possible answers.

Confusion means the player does not know what matters, cannot remember which term belongs to which problem, or discovers that an important choice depended on unstated information.

The game may temporarily disorient the protagonist through magic, but the interface and prose must still tell the player what the protagonist is trying to achieve.

## Information layers

### Layer One: required now

Facts needed to understand the current goal, danger, relationship, or cost. These belong in active prose and choice labels.

### Layer Two: useful context

Facts that deepen the place or suggest a better decision. These appear through optional questions, environmental detail, companion conversation, or a short journal entry.

### Layer Three: deep lore

History, cosmology, and faction detail that does not affect the current decision. It stays out of active prose until it becomes relevant. Interested players may discover it through optional scenes, but skipping it cannot make the main plot unclear.

## Terminology budget

- Introduce no more than one essential invented term in a normal scene.
- Introduce no more than three essential invented terms in a full chapter.
- Attach a new term to a concrete image or action when it first appears.
- Do not introduce two similar titles for the same role.
- Use a person's name more often than their title after the first introduction.
- Do not name a faction, spell, creature, and historical event in the same paragraph.
- A returning term receives a natural reminder if it has been absent for more than one chapter.

Example: the player first sees an iron pin changing the road. Only after understanding what it does does someone call it a World Nail. The wider set of nine is explained later.

## Mystery load

Each chapter carries:

- one active mystery the protagonist can investigate now;
- up to two background questions that remain quiet;
- one anticipated event such as reaching a city, meeting a person, or attempting a heist.

Do not place three active mysteries in one scene. When a new mystery becomes active, answer, narrow, or deliberately pause the previous one.

## The answer ladder

Every meaningful reveal performs four jobs in this order:

1. Answer a question the player has already formed.
2. Prove the answer through an action, object, or consequence.
3. Explain the minimum meaning needed now.
4. Create one larger question with a clear next step.

Characters should not deliver long explanations before the player has a reason to care.

## Mechanical onboarding

### First chapter

Teach one shared resource and two signature class ideas through low risk use before combat or crisis. Show the result immediately.

For Caelan, the player first uses Stamina, Guard, and Command during escort preparation. Oathfire arrives only near the final decision.

For Rook, the player first uses Dexterity, attention, and Heat during a controlled theft. Glimmer appears only after the ordinary plan fails.

For Ilyra, the player first uses Mana, Threadread, and a simple Ward during familiar community work. Memory Shards become available only after their personal meaning is understood.

### Later chapters

- Add no more than one new meter, currency, status family, or magical rule in a chapter.
- Use a mechanic safely once before requiring it under pressure.
- Remind the player of a rare mechanic through character thought or companion dialogue, not a manual page.
- Show costs on the choice before selection.
- After selection, show the resource change and its fictional effect together.
- Do not expose every hidden relationship or world value as a number.

## Player facing journal

The eventual game interface should offer a compact journal with five sections:

### Now

One sentence stating the current objective, the current location, and any urgent limit.

### What I Know

Short conclusions written in the protagonist's voice. Record confirmed facts separately from suspicions.

### People

Only recurring or currently relevant characters. Show the last meaningful change in the relationship rather than unexplained scores.

### Places

Locations the protagonist has visited or is actively travelling toward. Each entry includes one visual reminder and one unresolved local issue.

### Consequences

Named promises, debts, injuries, wanted status, altered routes, spent memories, and regional outcomes that the player can reasonably remember.

The journal is not a lore encyclopedia. It exists to restore context after time away from the game.

## Returning player recap

When a player returns after several days, show a dismissible recap of no more than eighty words:

- who the protagonist is;
- what just happened;
- the current objective;
- one important cost or promise still active.

Never recap the entire cosmology. Never reveal information the current route has not discovered.

## Choice presentation

Choice text describes intent in the protagonist's voice.

Good Caelan choice: Hold the wagon while Mara gets the wounded clear. Costs 2 Stamina.

Good Rook choice: Let the guard see the coat, not the man inside it. Costs 1 Focus. Heat may rise.

Good Ilyra choice: Seal the memory until both fathers can be examined. Costs 2 Mana.

Avoid labels such as good choice, evil choice, romance choice, correct answer, or secret ending.

## Consequence communication

Use three signals:

- Immediate prose shows what physically or emotionally changed.
- A brief state message names a lasting consequence when the protagonist would recognise it.
- Later scenes call the consequence back through behaviour, access, injury, changed geography, or dialogue.

Do not display a message for trivial variables. Do not hide consequences that the protagonist would clearly understand.

## Character switching

Each new series begins with a self contained situation. The player does not need to remember the previous lead's inventory, supporting cast, or mechanics.

Imported world decisions appear first as visible conditions. Explanation follows only when the new protagonist investigates.

Example: Rook sees every lock open for one breath. The player acts first. Later dialogue explains how Caelan's Gate decision changed the law of promises.

When Ilyra's series begins, a short world state card gives no more than three consequences from Caelan and Rook. It avoids a full summary of their finales.

## Pacing and chapter length

Chapter count follows story pressure, not symmetry. A chapter exists only when it provides a distinct objective, location experience, irreversible decision, revelation, or relationship change.

Target play time may vary:

- settling or relationship chapter: twenty five to thirty five minutes;
- standard adventure chapter: thirty five to fifty minutes;
- major realm or finale chapter: fifty to seventy minutes with a clear midpoint break.

If a chapter contains two revelations that each change the player's goal, split it. If two chapters repeat the same emotional question without a new consequence, combine them.

## Series learning curves

### Caelan, twelve chapters

The player learns the ordinary world through duty and travel. The first three chapters explain folded roads before naming the wider system. The supernatural scale grows only after Caelan's human responsibilities are clear.

### Rook, eleven chapters

The player already knows the world is changing, so the mystery moves faster. Extra room is given to learning Brassreach and Serekh because both places change how stealth and deception work.

### Ilyra, thirteen chapters

The player enters with the most world knowledge, but Ilyra's magic and final choice are more complex. Her series separates evidence gathering, cultural consent, and the final spell so the player never learns a cosmological truth in the same moment they must judge it.

These counts are planning decisions, not permanent quotas. Testing may split or combine chapters when player understanding or emotional pacing demands it.

## Comprehension checkpoints

At the end of each authored chapter, conduct a blind playtest and ask:

1. What was your main goal?
2. Which person mattered most in this chapter and why?
3. What did your class let you do?
4. What did using that power cost?
5. What new fact do you now believe?
6. What do you expect or hope will happen next?

Do not explain answers during the test. If fewer than four of five players give the intended core answer to the first five questions, revise before adding more lore.

Different predictions for the sixth question are desirable if they are supported by the story.

## Emotional clarity

The player need not agree with the protagonist's companions, but should understand what each important person wants in the scene.

Before a betrayal, establish the relationship being betrayed. Before a sacrifice, establish what will be lost. Before intimacy, establish the immediate desire and any visible risk. Before a world decision, show at least one ordinary life affected by every major option.

## Final approval test

A chapter is ready only when:

- the objective can be stated in one sentence;
- the active mystery can be stated as one question;
- the new mechanic has been used safely before it becomes decisive;
- every new essential term is attached to a concrete event;
- the chapter answers something before adding a larger question;
- at least one consequence is visible without opening a menu;
- the final beat makes the next action desirable and understandable;
- playtesters are divided by anticipation, not by basic comprehension.
